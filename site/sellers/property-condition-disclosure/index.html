New York property condition disclosure: what sellers must disclose now

By Bisendra Melaram, SRS, PSA, SRES, ABR, SFR, MRP, AHWD, RSPS, Licensed Real Estate Salesperson. Last updated November 8, 2026.

New York sellers of one to four family homes must give the buyer a signed property condition disclosure statement before the buyer signs the contract. Since March 20, 2024, sellers can no longer give a $500 credit instead, and the form asks eight flood questions, from FEMA flood zones and required insurance to past claims and federal flood aid.

Who has to complete the disclosure?

Sellers of one to four family homes used as a residence. Real Property Law section 462 excludes unimproved land, condominium units, cooperative apartments, and property in a homeowners association that the seller does not own in fee. Section 463 lists further exemptions, so ask your attorney whether one applies to you.

When is it due?

Before the buyer signs a binding contract of sale. The seller signs it and delivers it to the buyer or the buyer's agent, and a copy signed by both is attached to the contract.

What are the flood questions?

  1. Is any part of the property in a FEMA-designated floodplain?
  2. Is any part in a special flood hazard area, the 100-year floodplain, on current FEMA maps?
  3. Is any part in a moderate risk flood zone, the 500-year floodplain?
  4. Is the property subject to a federal requirement to carry flood insurance?
  5. Has the seller, or to the seller's knowledge a prior owner, received FEMA, SBA or other federal flood disaster aid?
  6. Is there flood insurance on the property? If so, a copy of the policy is attached.
  7. Is there a FEMA elevation certificate? If so, a copy is attached.
  8. Has a flood damage claim ever been filed with any insurer, including the National Flood Insurance Program?

Separate questions ask about flooding, drainage or grading problems that caused standing water, and whether the structure has had water seep in or flood damage. The form also notes that standard homeowner's policies usually do not cover flood damage.

What else does the form ask?

  • How long you have owned and lived in the home, and any rights others have to use the property.
  • Shared walls, fences or driveways, special assessments, and certificates of occupancy.
  • The age of the structure, with a lead paint note for homes built before 1978.
  • Fuel storage tanks, spills, landfill use, asbestos, lead plumbing, radon and mold testing.
  • Wetlands, rot, fire damage, pests, the roof and structural defects.
  • Water source, sewer or septic, electric service, and known defects in plumbing, heating, cooling, the foundation and other systems.
  • The school district the home is in.

Do you have to inspect your own home or research public records?

No. The statute says the disclosure is not a warranty and the seller has no duty to inspect or check public records. You answer from what you actually know, and you must give the buyer a revised statement if you learn something that makes an answer materially wrong, until title transfers or the buyer moves in.

How do you prepare your New York property condition disclosure?

  1. Look up your flood zone. Search your address on FEMA's Flood Map Service Center and note the zone.
  2. Gather flood records. Find your flood insurance policy, any elevation certificate, and any claim or aid records.
  3. Gather permits and certificates. Collect certificates of occupancy and permits for past work, and ask the building department about any open permits.
  4. Collect system records. Pull records on the roof, heating, fuel tanks, radon or mold tests and septic or sewer work.
  5. Answer from what you know. Complete each answer honestly, mark unknown where you do not know, and review it with your attorney before listing.

What else do people ask?

Can I still give the buyer a $500 credit instead of the disclosure?

No. Since March 20, 2024, New York sellers of most one to four family homes must complete the property condition disclosure statement. The $500 credit option is gone.

Does a condo or co-op seller fill out the disclosure?

Not this one. New York Real Property Law section 462 excludes condominium units and cooperative apartments from the property condition disclosure statement.

Do I have to hire an inspector before I answer?

No. The statute says the seller has no duty to inspect the property or search public records. You answer from your actual knowledge.

What if my answers change after the buyer signs?

If you learn something that makes an answer materially inaccurate, you must deliver a revised statement as soon as practicable, up until title transfers or the buyer moves in.
